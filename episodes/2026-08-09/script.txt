Sunday, August 9th. Here is your pork industry news.

Today we're tracking a fresh wave of African swine fever news out of Europe and Asia. We've got a steep slide in U.S. hog prices this week. And we'll cover new trade friction over tariffs, plus a major legal settlement for Tyson Foods.

Let's start with disease and biosecurity. Finland has confirmed its first-ever African swine fever case, found in a wild boar. The discovery was made on July 30th in Virolahti, near the Russian border. The Finnish Food Authority has set up an infection zone and restricted pig movement in the area. Finland had stayed disease-free for years even as neighbors like Poland, Estonia, and Latvia battled outbreaks. Officials are now surveying the wild boar population to gauge how far the virus has spread. The case adds a new concern to the European Union's broader containment strategy.

South Korea has also confirmed its first farm-level African swine fever outbreak of 2026. The country has been fighting the disease since 2019, and farm-level cases carry a heavy economic cost. Authorities have imposed strict movement restrictions and carried out preventive culling on nearby farms. The outbreak keeps pressure on neighboring countries, including Japan, to watch their import controls closely.

Estonia is still reeling from a recent wave of African swine fever outbreaks. About 10 percent of the country's total hog herd, roughly 25,000 animals, has been lost. Eleven farm outbreaks led to the culling of 62,000 hogs in total. Estonia's pork self-sufficiency rate has fallen from 72 percent to 50 percent as a result. The government has now unveiled a 75-million-euro plan to rebuild the industry, funding six new farms. The idea is to consolidate smaller operations into larger facilities with stronger biosecurity. Full production recovery is expected to take at least one to two years.

More broadly across Europe, a report from the UK's Animal and Plant Health Agency tracked the disease this year. African swine fever was detected in 16 European Union countries in just the first two months of 2026. In Germany's North Rhine-Westphalia state, the wild boar outbreak keeps expanding. It's now within about 150 kilometers of the Belgian-Dutch border. Spain's outbreak, confirmed back in November of 2025, triggered export restrictions that are still weighing on the market. Those restrictions have left the European Union with a domestic pork surplus and falling prices. The European Commission now forecasts full-year 2026 EU pork production will shrink about 1 percent, to 21.76 million metric tons. A shrinking sow herd and African swine fever-linked export limits are both cited as causes. EU pork exports are expected to fall a further 3 percent this year. Even so, the bloc's self-sufficiency rate is expected to hold steady at around 115 percent.

Turning to U.S. prices and supply, PorkCheckoff's weekly price summary for the week ending July 31st put the carcass price at 103 dollars and 3 cents per hundredweight. Ribs led all cuts at 166 dollars and 2 cents, with bellies close behind at 157 dollars and 98 cents. Butts came in at 111 dollars and 84 cents, hams at 97 dollars and 38 cents, and loins at 89 dollars and 96 cents. That strength came just after the peak of summer grilling season. Analysts are watching whether softer seasonal demand and rising fall supply start to pull prices down.

That pullback has already begun. USDA's hog report for August 5th put the national negotiated carcass price at 98 dollars and 91 cents per hundredweight, down 52 cents from the day before. The Western Corn Belt came in at 99 dollars and 51 cents, and the Eastern Corn Belt at 96 dollars and 15 cents. That's a drop of more than 4 dollars in just five days from the July 31st reading. It's a clear sign that seasonal demand is easing and putting downward pressure on the market.

Pork Business reports that packer margins are sliding deeper into negative territory, even as producer profits stay relatively steady. Packers are being squeezed between high live hog costs and softer product prices. Producers, meanwhile, are benefiting from low feed costs alongside still-solid cash hog prices. That's shifting profit along the supply chain away from packers and toward producers. If the trend continues, it could pressure packers to push hog prices lower or scale back plant run rates.

On the demand side, National Hog Farmer reports that U.S. pork exports are expected to rise 3 percent in 2026, reaching 7.19 billion pounds. Loin exports are playing an outsized role, now making up 47 percent of shipments outside the USMCA region. With the breeding herd not expanding, supply is staying relatively tight. Combined with strong export demand, that should help support prices even after the summer peak has passed.

Now let's turn to the Chinese market. AHDB reports that China's pork imports fell 26.4 percent year over year in the first half of 2026, to 390,000 tons. Including pork offal, total imports were down 14.8 percent. Rising domestic production, up 4.2 percent in the first quarter, is a key driver. Chinese consumers are also shifting toward cheaper protein sources like eggs and tofu. The European Union remains China's top pork supplier, even as anti-dumping duties complicate the picture. AHDB expects Chinese imports to recover gradually in the second half as domestic supply tightens.

Separately, Pig333 reports that China's domestic pork production kept expanding in the first half of 2026, supported by ample supply. Chinese import volumes were down about 29 percent year over year over the same period. Farm-level hog prices in China have continued to decline, though government stockpile purchases are offering some support. Major exporters like the United States and the European Union are continuing to see shrinking access to the Chinese market, making alternative markets increasingly important.

On trade and tariffs, USMEF reports that U.S. pork exports to China fell 9 percent year over year in the first quarter of 2026, to 104,779 metric tons. Export value dropped 20 percent, to 222.9 million dollars. Chinese retaliatory tariffs and ample domestic Chinese supply are the main causes. U.S. exporters are shifting focus toward Mexico, Latin America, and Japan instead. Trade talks with the European Union are also underway, with preferential market access for U.S. pork on the table.

There's also positive movement on a narrower trade front. Restrictions on U.S. pork byproducts tied to pseudorabies concerns in Mexico have now been eased, according to a report dated August 7th. USMEF had estimated the restriction was costing about 7 million dollars a week in lost export opportunity. Shipments of pork bellies and offal to Mexico are resuming as a result. Weekly export sales for the week of July 30th came in at 27,335 metric tons, a three-week low. Still, Mexico, at 16,500 metric tons, and Japan, at 3,500 metric tons, both led the way.

Pork Business also reports that the U.S. pork industry is voicing strong concern over the threat of new tariffs. Industry groups warn of eroding export competitiveness and the risk of losing market share. Pork exports to China have already dropped sharply under current trade conditions. Groups are calling for faster progress on multilateral trade negotiations. Delays to the Farm Bill are adding to the uncertainty, along with expanding state-level compliance costs tied to Proposition 12 animal welfare rules. Taken together, the industry is facing a highly uncertain profit outlook for the second half of 2026.

In Japan, Nokei Shimbun reports that the halt on Spanish pork imports, in place since November of 2025 due to African swine fever, has now lasted more than five months. Spain accounted for about 30 percent of Japan's frozen pork imports back in 2024, making it the country's top supplier. Japanese buyers are now shifting to Brazil, the United States, and other European Union suppliers instead. Domestic Japanese carcass prices in April were up nearly 30 percent year over year as a result. Ham and sausage makers are facing higher input costs and are looking for ways to manage the increase.

On the legal and packer front, Tyson Foods has agreed to pay 85 million dollars to settle a pork price-fixing lawsuit. The case alleged that Tyson used data shared through Agri Stats to help limit hog supply. It's the largest single settlement in more than seven years of related antitrust litigation. Combined with earlier settlements from companies including JBS and Hormel, total consumer recovery now stands at 208 million dollars. That tops Smithfield's 75-million-dollar settlement from 2022. The case adds to legal and reputational pressure across the industry, and it's likely to bring closer scrutiny to data-sharing practices like Agri Stats going forward.

In other developments, a separate AHDB update on the U.S. market echoed this week's price data, noting steady demand but limited production growth. AHDB's separate China market report put first-half imports at 383,000 tons, down 28.9 percent, with June alone falling to 67,762 tons, the lowest June total since 2019. Six state pork associations in the southeastern United States have formed a new Southeast Pork Alliance to coordinate policy advocacy. That includes a unified push on Farm Bill priorities and Proposition 12 compliance. USDA trade data for June showed beef exports outperforming pork, though the temporary pork offal restriction behind that gap has since been resolved. National Hog Farmer reports that USDA now expects 2026 U.S. pork production to rise 2.3 percent, to 28.215 billion pounds. Heavier average hog weights are offsetting a smaller breeding herd. USDA's average live hog price forecast for the year stands at 67 dollars per hundredweight. And Barchart reports that CME lean hog futures, which track live hog prices, traded lower on Thursday, August 7th. That came alongside a national hog price of 98 dollars and 30 cents per hundredweight, down 71 cents on the day.

That's your pork industry news for today.
