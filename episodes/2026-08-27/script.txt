Thursday, August 27th. Here is your pork industry news. Today we cover U.S. hog prices under pressure, a new fifty percent U.S.-Canada tariff shaking the cross-border pig trade, and weak hog prices squeezing packer earnings in Denmark and China. We'll also touch on feed costs, animal health, welfare policy, and a sustainability milestone out of Spain. Let's get into it.

Let's start with prices. On August 26th, the pork carcass composite price fell 2 dollars and 12 cents to 95 dollars and 55 cents per hundredweight. That's according to Brownfield Ag News. October lean hog futures on the CME edged higher that same day. The contract added 45 cents to close at 80 dollars and 90 cents. December lean hogs also gained ground, up 62 cents to close at 70 dollars and 97 cents. In the cash market, the national weighted average price for barrows and gilts slipped 27 cents to 90 dollars and 75 cents. Regional numbers told a similar story of modest softening. Iowa and southern Minnesota hogs came in a bit lower, at 90 dollars and 72 cents. The western Corn Belt averaged 90 dollars and 78 cents. Estimated hog slaughter rose 13,000 head from the previous week, reaching 484,000 head for the day. That uptick in slaughter numbers came even as cash and carcass prices moved lower.

The day before, on August 25th, USDA put the pork carcass cutout at 97 dollars and 62 cents per hundredweight. That was down 2 dollars and 31 cents from the prior session, The Pig Site reported. The most active October lean hog contract on the CME slipped that day too, falling about two-thirds of a cent to 80.45 cents per pound. Cattle markets were soft on the same day. October live cattle fell 2.65 cents to 210.95 cents per pound. September feeder cattle dropped 4.95 cents to 319.275 cents per pound. So across both hogs and cattle, it was a weaker session for livestock futures.

Turning to feed costs, Chicago soybeans climbed 1.10 percent on August 26th. U.S. crop conditions worsened, and that added upward pressure on feed costs, Suinocultura Industrial reported. Looking further out, USDA projects Brazil's 2026-27 soybean crop at 186 million tons. On the demand side, China's August purchases of U.S. soybeans totaled just 500,000 tons, a modest figure that market watchers will be tracking closely in the weeks ahead. In Brazil, the second-crop corn harvest reached 92 percent of planted area. With that harvest progressing well, feed supplies for pigs and poultry are expected to remain adequate over the coming months, even as soybean prices tick higher.

Now to trade, where the biggest story of the week is unfolding along the U.S.-Canada border. The United States invoked Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act on August 22nd. That move imposed an immediate 50 percent tariff on about 20 billion dollars of Canadian goods, Suinocultura Industrial reported. Canada responded on August 25th, announcing it will apply retaliatory tariffs of the same scale. Those retaliatory measures take effect September 8th. Bilateral trade talks between the two countries were formally suspended on August 21st, just before the tariff took hold. The stakes for the hog industry are significant, given how closely the U.S. and Canadian pork sectors are linked. Manitoba exports more than 3 million weaned pigs to the United States every year. About 2 million of those pigs are shipped directly to Iowa. With tariffs now in place and talks suspended, there is growing concern about disruption to that cross-border pork supply chain. Producers on both sides of the border are watching closely to see whether the two governments return to the table.

On the export side, Brazil's pork exports rose 3.7 percent in July, reaching 131,500 tons. That figure comes from a headline-only report out of 3tres3.com, picked up via Google News. Pig333 noted that the full article was not yet available. Details on the comparison period and on destination breakdowns were not confirmed at the time of this report. We'll bring you the fuller picture on that story as it becomes available.

On the corporate side, Denmark's FirstFarms delivered a rough set of first-half results. The company reported that first-half 2026 EBITDA and EBIT worsened significantly despite higher revenue, The Pig Site reported. Weak hog and milk prices were the main drag on performance. Hog prices alone cut earnings by 16 million Danish kroner. A drought in Slovakia added to the pressure, leaving crop yields 35 percent below budget, a significant shortfall for the company's crop operations there. In response, FirstFarms cut its full-year guidance. The company now expects EBITDA of negative 5 million to negative 45 million kroner. It expects EBIT of negative 75 million to negative 25 million kroner.

In China, COFCO Joycome Foods swung to a first-half 2026 net loss of 1.09 billion yuan. That compares with a profit in the same period a year earlier, The Pig Site reported. The average selling price for finishing hogs fell 29.6 percent year over year, and that decline weighed heavily on results. Revenue also dropped, down 2.5 percent from a year earlier. There was a bright spot: the company's branded business grew, making up 3.5 percentage points more of revenue than a year earlier. Even so, that gain wasn't enough to offset the drop in hog prices, and the company's bottom line still swung to a loss.

Turning to policy and animal welfare, the U.S. Government Accountability Office has called on Congress to modernize livestock transport law. National Hog Farmer reported the news on August 26th. The same report noted that the American Association of Swine Veterinarians backs the National Pork Board's Transport Quality Assurance program. That program trains and certifies hog handlers across the country.

Also from National Hog Farmer, funding for U.S. animal activist groups has now topped 1.07 billion dollars. A newly formed group is targeting language in the draft 2026 Farm Bill that supports modern pork production. That group is running protests at restaurants and retailers nationwide as it pushes to change the bill's language. The growing war chest signals that pressure on pork industry policy is likely to continue.

In animal health news, Uruguay has formally joined BANVACO. That's the regional foot-and-mouth disease vaccine and antigen bank coordinated by PANAFTOSA and the Pan American Health Organization, Pig333 reported. Uruguay becomes the fourth member of the bank, following Brazil, Ecuador, and Paraguay. The membership agreement was announced August 12th. It was signed during a two-day workshop focused on health status and market protection. Membership gives the region faster access to emergency vaccines covering various strains if an outbreak occurs, strengthening the collective defense against the disease.

And in the European Union, a new product has earned quality protection. Poland's Kielbasa pradziada z Dukli sausage has been registered as a Protected Geographical Indication. It's the 51st Polish product to earn EU quality status, Pig333 reported. The sausage is made from the meat and liver of Pulawska breed pigs or their crossbreeds. Its fat content is kept at 5 percent or below. Poland's government announced the registration on August 6th, adding another regional specialty to the EU's protected foods list.

Finally, on sustainability, Spain's livestock sector made measurable progress on emissions. Ammonia emissions from the sector fell 2.3 percent in 2024 compared with the prior year. That's according to the Spanish Emissions Inventory, as reported by Pig333. Measured against the 2005 baseline, emissions are now down 17.6 percent. Emissions from manure management fell 14.9 percent over that period. Emissions from organic fertilizer application dropped 2.1 percent. Across all of Spain, overall emissions have declined 13 percent since 2005. That means the country has now met EU reduction targets for four consecutive years. Spain's Ecogan system tracks farm-by-farm adoption of best available techniques for both intensive pig and poultry operations. That gives regulators a clear, farm-level view of progress.

That's your pork industry news for today.
