Monday, August 3rd. Here is your pork industry news.

Today we've got a major processing investment from Smithfield. There are fresh African swine fever outbreaks in Poland and Germany. We also have a bleak trade outlook for European pork exporters. And we'll close with the latest word on U.S. hog prices and Chinese import demand.

Let's start with packer developments. Smithfield Foods is owned by China's WH Group. The company announced it will invest up to 1.3 billion dollars over the next three years. The money will go toward a new pork and packaged meat processing plant in Sioux Falls, South Dakota. Production is expected to begin by late 2028. Smithfield says it aims to make the facility the most advanced pork plant in the United States. This Chinese-backed investment marks a major expansion of processing capacity on American soil. It points to a broader reshaping of the North American pork supply chain. Analysts expect automation and efficiency gains at the new plant to lower production costs. That could shift competitive dynamics across the industry in the years ahead.

Also on the packer side, Hormel Foods announced a leadership change. John Ghingo has been named the company's next president and CEO. His appointment takes effect October 26th, 2026. Ghingo brings more than 25 years of consumer goods leadership experience. He rejoined Hormel in 2024 and has been leading the company's retail segment, its largest business unit. Hormel posts annual sales of 12 billion dollars across more than 30 brands, including SPAM. Industry watchers will be looking closely at how the new CEO shapes the company's product portfolio. His strategic moves on market positioning will also draw attention in the months ahead.

Turning to disease and biosecurity, Poland has confirmed its first African swine fever outbreak of 2026. It hit a commercial farm, and it's a big one. The infected farm holds 21,390 animals. That makes it the second-largest ASF-affected farm in Poland since 2014. Poland continues to lead the European Union in ASF cases, with 1,425 confirmed outbreaks to date. The virus also continues to spread among wild boar. There have been 1,241 cumulative wild boar cases recorded so far. This latest outbreak raises the risk of tighter export restrictions on Polish pork. That adds to supply uncertainty for both EU exporters and importers.

Germany is seeing a mixed picture on ASF. Overall case numbers are declining nationally. But a new outbreak was confirmed in early July in the Uckermark district of Brandenburg. It was the first wild boar infection there since May of 2025. In Saxony, cumulative infected wild boar have now reached 71. Germany's total ASF case count stands at 621, the fourth highest in Europe. There has been no spread into domestic pig herds so far. Still, the continued circulation of the virus among wild boar remains a risk. It could still threaten German pork exports down the line.

Now let's look at trade and tariffs. A USDA outlook, reported by S&P Global, expects European Union pork exports to fall 8 percent in 2026. The drop reflects the combined impact of African swine fever and Chinese tariffs. China's final tariff on EU pork is set at 4.9 to 19.8 percent for five years. That tariff has already reshaped trade flows significantly. China's share of EU pork exports has collapsed from 54 percent in 2020 to just 18 percent in 2025. EU pork production is also forecast to decline in 2026, to 21.6 million tonnes. The ASF outbreak in Spain adds further downside risk to that outlook. EU exporters are racing to diversify into new markets. The Philippines, South Korea, Taiwan, and Vietnam are all seeing more attention from EU shippers.

On the U.S. side, the National Pork Producers Council is pushing for stronger USMCA protections. The Trump administration passed on a long-term renewal of the agreement. Instead, officials opted for a 10-year framework with annual reviews. NPPC held its third round of trade talks on the matter in Mexico City. Mexico and Canada together account for 33 percent of U.S. pork exports. That trade is worth 2.8 billion dollars to Mexico and 750 million dollars to Canada. Combined, it's more than 3.5 billion dollars in pork trade with the two neighbors. NPPC warns that the annual review structure introduces fresh negotiating uncertainty every single year. The council calls that a significant risk for pork exporters. NPPC is urging officials to preserve market access and tariff preferences as talks continue.

There's better news on the export front from USMEF. U.S. pork exports were up 17 percent year to date through April. Volume reached 123,458 tons, with export value up 11 percent over the same period. Japan has been a standout market this year. Shipments to Japan were up more than 20 percent year over year as of April. That pace is nearing the highest level for Japan since 2016. Growth is being driven by rising demand for convenience foods in Japan. Supply constraints among competing exporters are also helping U.S. shipments. The tariff advantage the U.S. holds under the CPTPP agreement is another factor. USMEF says 2026 could turn out to be a record year for U.S. pork exports. That export strength is also helping support domestic hog prices, USMEF notes.

Shifting to the Chinese market, AHDB reports a sharp pullback in Chinese import demand. China's pork and pork byproduct imports fell 30 percent year over year between January and May of 2026. That put volume at 314,000 tons for the period. At the same time, domestic Chinese production rose 4.2 percent in the first quarter. That brought output to 16.7 million tonnes, pushing the country closer to self-sufficiency. Beijing has already carried out one round of frozen pork reserve purchases. The goal was to support producer returns amid falling prices. Reports suggest a second, larger round of reserve buying may be coming. The sharp drop in Chinese import demand is hitting major exporters hard. Both the EU and the United States are feeling the impact. The search for alternative markets is becoming increasingly urgent across the industry.

Finally, let's turn to U.S. prices and supply. AHDB's latest market update finds domestic demand holding steady. Production growth, meanwhile, remains limited for now. Competition from lower-cost chicken is capping how much pork prices can rise. AHDB forecasts full-year 2026 U.S. pork prices up 2.4 percent versus last year. That would be a modest but steady increase. Rabobank expects prices to stay broadly stable through the second half of 2026. No major swings in either direction are expected, according to the bank.

That stability hasn't shown up in the futures market just yet. CME's daily hog market report shows August lean hog futures falling to a two-week low. Demand weakness persisted, and average hog weights continued to climb. The pork carcass cutout value edged up slightly to 104 dollars and 63 cents per hundredweight. Futures traders showed little reaction to that move. Soft demand is still weighing heavily on market sentiment. Falling grain prices have been encouraging producers to feed hogs to heavier weights. That's adding further downward pressure on the supply side. A Midwest summer heat wave could reduce hog weights and tighten supply somewhat. For now, though, the market remains capped on the upside.

National Hog Farmer strikes a similar note about a disappointing summer for U.S. pork prices. Lean hog futures have been trading in a range of 96 to 100 dollars per hundredweight. That's below what many in the industry had expected heading into summer. USDA's pork carcass cutout was reported at 104 dollars and 4 cents per hundredweight. The grilling season has failed to lift prices much this year. Falling grain costs have pushed the average hog weight up to 286.9 pounds. That extra weight is adding to oversupply and weighing on the market. Traders are now watching for a possible demand recovery later in the third quarter.

That's your pork industry news for today.
