Wednesday, August 26th. Here is your pork industry news.

Today we cover the latest hog price and futures action out of Chicago. We also look at a wave of farm distress in the United Kingdom. And we cover rising feed costs tied to a shrinking US corn crop, plus a major digital farming push from Brazil's JBS.

Let's start with prices and the futures market. On August 25th, CME October lean hog futures fell 0.67 cent to close at 80.45 cents per pound. December lean hogs fell 1.12 cents to 70.35 cents. Remember, the lean hog contract tracks live hog prices, not pork meat prices. Cash hogs told a different story that day. National daily direct trade for barrows and gilts rose 1 dollar and 62 cents, with a base range of 84 to 95 dollars and 25 cents per hundredweight. The weighted average came in at 91 dollars and 2 cents, with the Western Corn Belt at 90 dollars and 69 cents and the Eastern Corn Belt at 94 dollars and 37 cents. The pork cutout price fell 2 dollars and 26 cents to 97 dollars and 67 cents. Estimated slaughter came in at 488,000 head, up 12,000 from the previous week and up about 9,000 from a year ago. That's according to Brownfield Ag News.

A day earlier, on August 24th, USDA's wholesale pork carcass price rose 1 dollar and 97 cents to 99 dollars and 93 cents per hundredweight. CME October lean hogs that day were up a quarter cent to 81 point 125 cents per pound. The Pig Site reports that the cattle market fell that same day. USDA had begun a phased reopening of the border with Mexico, which had been closed for more than a year due to New World screwworm. October live cattle fell 4 point 325 cents to 213 point 60 cents, and September feeder cattle dropped 4 dollars and 80 cents to 324 point 225 cents. USDA plans to open two more crossing points in New Mexico in 30 and 60 days.

Turning to the supply outlook, Rabobank says the US pork market saw prices pressured in the second quarter of 2026 by more market-ready hogs and softer than expected demand. The bank sees signs of stabilization heading into the third quarter. Weekly slaughter in July ran about 3 percent below year ago levels, reflecting earlier disease-related losses and routine plant maintenance shutdowns. Rabobank expects US hog slaughter numbers to keep running below year ago levels through the second half of 2026. Producers are holding sow herds steady while raising market weights to keep output up. Disease risk, export uncertainty, and cautious consumer demand are keeping them from expanding further, according to Rabobank.

USDA's latest cold storage report showed pork inventories rising overall even as beef stocks declined. Pork belly stocks fell 31 percent from the previous month but were up 14 percent from a year earlier. Figures for other individual pork categories were not available from the source, according to National Hog Farmer.

In Brazil, Cepea data show live hog prices in Sao Paulo state falling to the third lowest level in that price series. Specific price figures were not available from the source. Cepea notes that pork demand typically softens in the second half of the month, as buying power weakens.

In the United Kingdom, AHDB's August 25th market update says pork production stayed at elevated levels in the second quarter of 2026. Only the headline figure was available, with no specific production numbers given.

In Belgium, statistics agency Statbel reports the Flanders region's hog herd fell 3 percent in 2025 to 4.9 million head. That's down from around 5.8 million head as recently as 2016 through 2020. Sow numbers in Flanders are now 43 percent below where they stood in 2005.

Now to feed costs. As of August 24th, Chicago soybean futures were up 3 percent for the week, driven mainly by expectations of a lower US yield. Reports pointed to a 17 million ton drop in US corn production. Suinocultura Industrial reports that feed millers are now prioritizing short term hedging, diversifying suppliers, and managing existing inventory over buying in bulk. In Brazil, export data from Secex showed 218 point 6 billion dollars and 490 point 1 million tons shipped from January 1st through August 8th. The commercial dollar exchange rate, known as PTAX, stood at 5 point 1625. Brazil's development bank, BNDES, is investing 42 point 8 million reais to expand storage capacity at Coasul in Parana state.

Turning to trade and policy, the National Pork Producers Council welcomed a USDA announcement that it will purchase American pork for federal food assistance programs. The purchases are made under Section 32 of the 1935 Agricultural Adjustment Act, a fund built from US tariff revenue that supports commodity purchases and disaster relief. NPPC President Rob Brenneman said on August 24th that getting pork to schools and food banks is an important step given difficult market conditions. USDA regularly buys pork for federal food programs including school lunches, though the volume and value of this purchase were not disclosed.

In France, a sweeping new agricultural law took effect after being signed on August 18th and published in the official gazette the next day. The Emergency Law for the Protection and Sovereignty of Agriculture contains 55 articles. It bans imports of food containing residues of substances or veterinary medicines that are banned within the European Union. It requires public food service providers to prioritize EU sourced products. It calls for doubling French agricultural water storage capacity by 2035. It also simplifies livestock registration and permitting rules and reforms the country's animal disease prevention, surveillance, and crisis management systems. The law aims to strengthen producer organizations and introduce pricing mechanisms that reflect production costs, to improve farmer bargaining power and income.

Meanwhile, seventeen British agricultural organizations issued a joint statement on August 25th calling for emergency government support. The group includes the National Farmers Union and the National Pig Association. The UK is facing its worst drought in 50 years, which has caused record crop losses. The pork sector is separately under pressure from Europe wide oversupply, weak prices, and an unusually high number of contract terminations by packers this year. That combination is pushing producers to cut breeding herds or leave the industry altogether. The groups are asking for an interest free loan program tied to drought losses, called the Keep Britain Growing Loan. They also want a new scheme to help cover the cost of collecting and disposing of livestock affected by bluetongue disease.

On the corporate and packer side, Brazil's JBS, through its Seara subsidiary, began rolling out a digital farm management platform called SuperAgroTech in August. It's aimed at more than 9,000 contracted poultry and hog farmers who supply Seara exclusively. The platform moves farm operations and paperwork to the cloud, and it offers e-commerce purchasing of supplies and equipment. It also provides preferential credit terms and enables real time farm monitoring. Seara ranks farmers on a new Seara Score, which weighs farm performance, creditworthiness, and social and environmental measures like water use and waste management. Farmers who score well get access to expanded credit lines and e-commerce discounts. Seara is piloting IoT sensors on farms that supply its plants in Forquilhinha in Santa Catarina state and Montenegro in Rio Grande do Sul state. It plans to install the sensors across all contracted farms by next year.

Also in the packer world, Hormel Foods announced on August 24th that it has named Ash Bhumbla as its new Chief Financial Officer. Bhumbla previously served as CFO of Tyson Foods' chicken and international divisions. He takes over in September from Paul Kuenneman, who had held the interim CFO role since October of 2025. The move follows last month's announcement that John Ghingo would become Hormel's new CEO, continuing a broader shakeup of the company's leadership. Hormel is scheduled to report third quarter earnings on Thursday.

That's your pork industry news for today.
