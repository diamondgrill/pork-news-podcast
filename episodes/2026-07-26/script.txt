Sunday, July 26th. Here is your pork industry news. Today we cover a new 50 percent tariff on Canadian goods, a sharp drop in Chinese pork imports, record retail pork prices in Japan, and a historic settlement in a U.S. price-fixing lawsuit. It's a busy day for the global pork trade, with developments touching prices, disease, tariffs, and litigation all at once. We'll take these one at a time, starting with the numbers coming out of the hog and pork markets this week.

Let's start with prices and supply.

According to Barchart, CME lean hog futures eased slightly on Friday, July 25th. Remember, lean hog futures track live hog prices, not pork meat prices directly, so the name refers to the hog itself. Traders were locking in profits ahead of the weekend after a strong run earlier in the week. The August contract held in the 102 dollar range even with Friday's pullback. That means the week's gains were largely preserved heading into the weekend, a sign of continued strength in the futures market.

On the cash and retail side, USDA's national average carcass base price, reported July 24th, came in at 95 dollars and 87 cents per hundredweight. The net average price, which factors in additional adjustments on top of the base price, was 97 dollars and 83 cents per hundredweight. Both figures point to a cash hog market that remains firm heading into late summer.

Retail pork prices climbed to 5 dollars and 1 cent per pound. That is the highest retail price level recorded since October of 2023, a sign that strength at the farm level is showing up clearly on grocery store shelves for American shoppers.

Feed costs, meanwhile, are holding steady. September corn futures traded around 4 dollars and 64 cents. August soybean futures traded around 12 dollars and 38 cents. With feed costs stable and hog prices firm, the overall margin environment for producers continues to look favorable heading into late summer. That combination of firm hog prices and steady feed costs is a welcome setup for producers as the year moves into its second half.

Now let's turn to Japan, where retail pork prices have hit a record high. The national average retail price for loin-equivalent pork reached 287 yen per 100 grams in July. That is the highest level ever recorded for this benchmark, surpassing all previous readings.

Two forces are behind the jump. First, a weaker yen is pushing up the cost of imported pork, since a large share of Japan's pork supply comes from overseas. Second, an outbreak of African Swine Fever in Spain has forced a halt to imports of Spanish pork, tightening supply just as demand remains steady among Japanese consumers.

Japan's projected pork imports for July stand at 87,200 tons. That works out to about 105.5 percent of last year's volume for the same month, so overall import volume is not shrinking. Instead, the mix of supplier countries is shifting. Importers are turning to American and Brazilian pork to make up for the Spanish supply that has been cut off by the disease outbreak. For now, cost pressures on Japanese consumers are expected to continue, with no clear signal yet of when prices might ease. Together, the currency effect and the Spanish supply disruption are combining to push Japan's pork prices to territory not seen before.

Let's move to tariffs and trade, where there's a major development out of Washington.

President Trump imposed an additional 50 percent tariff on Canadian goods on July 21st. This is a broad measure. It covers products that would normally fall under the USMCA trade agreement, the trade pact linking the United States, Mexico, and Canada. The tariff is set to take effect on August 19th, giving businesses roughly a month to prepare.

Canada is one of the largest trading partners for U.S. live hogs and pork, so this tariff is being watched closely across the industry. Analysts say the new tariff creates a real risk of disruption to meat flows between the two countries, given how closely integrated the U.S. and Canadian hog sectors have become. If Canada responds with retaliatory tariffs of its own, the entire North American pork supply chain could face pressure to reorganize how hogs and pork move across the border. Industry participants on both sides of the border will be watching closely in the weeks ahead to see whether Canada responds in kind. All eyes are now on whether the two governments can soften the impact before the tariff takes effect on August 19th.

Also on the trade front, pig333 reports that pork imports into China fell sharply in the first half of 2026. Total pork imports came to 390,000 tons, down 26.4 percent from 530,000 tons in the same period a year earlier. When you include offal, combined pork and offal imports totaled 980,000 tons, down 14.8 percent year over year. That is a significant pullback in demand from what has long been one of the world's largest pork import markets.

Two factors are driving that decline. Domestic pork production inside China has been rising, reducing the need for imported supply. And in December of 2025, China imposed anti-dumping tariffs on pork from the European Union, with rates ranging from 4.9 percent to 19.8 percent. That has made EU pork considerably more expensive for Chinese buyers, and it helps explain why European shipments have slowed.

That said, there are signs the picture is shifting month to month. Some reports indicate that China's pork imports jumped to 430,000 tons in July alone, a single-month surge well above the first-half monthly average. That suggests Chinese buyers are diversifying their supplier base rather than simply importing less overall. Demand that once went to European Union pork appears to be moving toward American and Brazilian pork instead. It's a trend worth watching closely in the months ahead as the full picture develops.

Finally, let's cover a major packer and legal development.

According to Food Dive, Tyson Foods has agreed to an 85 million dollar settlement in a pork price-fixing lawsuit. That case has been in litigation for more than seven years and also involved JBS and Smithfield as defendants alongside Tyson.

This is the largest settlement reached in this litigation to date, and industry watchers are calling it the highest settlement figure in the case's history. It surpasses the 75 million dollar settlement that Smithfield agreed to in the same case back in late 2022, which had previously stood as the largest payout among the defendants. Each new settlement in this case has raised the bar, and Tyson's agreement now sets a fresh high-water mark for the litigation as a whole.

A final approval hearing for the Tyson settlement is scheduled for June 2026. Beyond the dollar figure itself, the case has already fueled broader discussion across the industry about pricing practices among major pork packers. It could also lead to closer regulatory scrutiny of competition within the packing sector going forward. Regulators and industry observers are expected to keep following the case as it moves toward final approval. With three of the industry's largest packers now having reached settlements, the litigation stands as one of the most closely watched antitrust cases the U.S. pork sector has seen.

That wraps up today's major stories. U.S. hog prices stayed firm and feed costs held steady, keeping producer margins in good shape. Retail pork prices in Japan hit a record, driven by the weak yen and African Swine Fever in Spain. Washington imposed a new 50 percent tariff on Canadian goods, set to take effect next month. China's pork imports fell sharply in the first half of the year, though July alone showed a possible rebound as buyers diversify their suppliers. And Tyson Foods agreed to a record 85 million dollar settlement in the pork price-fixing case, the largest payout yet among the defendants.

That's your pork industry news for today.
