Wednesday, July 29th. Here is your pork industry news. Today we cover USDA's latest production outlook, firm hog prices and futures, record levels of disease outbreaks worldwide, and fresh movement on the U.S.-China tariff front. We'll also look at a major new packing plant investment and check conditions in the European Union and China. Let's start with prices and supply.

According to National Hog Farmer, USDA's July World Agricultural Supply and Demand Estimates report trimmed its outlook for U.S. pork production in 2026 and 2027. The 2026 production forecast was revised down slightly to 27.955 billion pounds. The average price for barrows and gilts was also revised down, to 64 dollars and 82 cents per hundredweight. That is a decrease of 1 dollar and 81 cents from the previous month's estimate. USDA said the downward revisions reflect ongoing disease risk and pressure from feed costs. The report also kept a cautious outlook for 2027 production, which is expected to weigh on the industry's medium-term planning. Producers are being urged to watch export trends, domestic demand, and policy shifts closely in the second half of the year.

Turning to the cash and futures markets, the pork carcass cutout value held at 104 dollars and 63 cents per hundredweight as of July 27th, up 15 cents from the previous day. Remember, CME lean hog futures track live hog prices, not pork meat prices, since the name refers to the hog breed itself. August lean hog futures traded firm, near 99.65 cents per pound, after touching a monthly high of 99.925 cents earlier in the month. The five-day cash hog average stood at 100 dollars and 46 cents per hundredweight. Summer demand and export flows are helping support the market. Attention now turns to how extreme summer heat affects hog shipments, and whether the strong cutout value can hold in the weeks ahead.

National Hog Farmer also reports that strong demand at home and abroad, combined with slower growth in supply, has pushed U.S. hog prices higher through 2026. July's cutout value has remained above 100 dollars per hundredweight. Disease pressure continues to affect shipment numbers, hog weights, and slaughter flows, complicating price formation. Recovering exports to Japan, South Korea, and the Philippines are helping support prices. That raises the possibility that 2026 could turn out to be a record year for U.S. pork exports. Producers are benefiting from improved margins as prices rise, though disease and feed cost risks still need careful management.

A separate weekly pig report noted that lean hog futures have drifted higher as the market watches tight domestic supply. Export demand and the cash market have been moving together, pushing prices upward. Even with rising summer demand, growth in the number of hogs going to slaughter has stayed limited, keeping supply and demand tight. Strong export demand from Japan, South Korea, and the Philippines is again raising the possibility of a record export year in 2026. Cutout values and export flows will likely continue to set the tone in the weeks ahead.

Now let's turn to disease and biosecurity, where the global picture remains serious. According to Pork Business, outbreaks of African Swine Fever and foot-and-mouth disease have reached record levels worldwide. As of July 13th, more than 71 countries have been affected. Over 1.2 million African Swine Fever cases have been reported, and more than 2.4 million pigs have been lost. A new suspected case has also turned up in Kakamega County, Kenya, where samples are being collected for testing. This disease spread is having a major impact on supply outlooks in China, the European Union, and across Asia. Industry officials say stronger biosecurity measures are now an urgent priority. For producers and traders, disease risk remains a key variable shaping production and trade planning going forward.

In the European Union, regulators have updated their disease control measures. The European Commission issued Implementing Regulation 2026 slash 1795 on July 16th, updating the restricted zones under its African Swine Fever special measures. The update responds to new outbreaks in Spain, Hungary, Poland, and Slovakia, revising the zones that restrict movement of pork and live hogs within the EU. Since the outbreak in Spain back in November of 2025, a large volume of Spanish pork has remained inside the EU rather than being exported. That has added to supply pressure across the bloc and weighed on export competitiveness. Producers and exporters in the affected countries are being urged to check the newly designated zones and adjust accordingly.

Let's move to tariffs and trade, where there's significant movement on the U.S.-China front. According to National Hog Farmer, progress on U.S.-China tariff modifications marks a key step toward restoring U.S. pork's access to the Chinese market. If China halts and removes the retaliatory tariffs it has applied since March 4th of 2025, American pork's competitiveness in China would recover significantly. In 2025, U.S. pork and variety meat exports to China totaled 367,416 metric tons, down 21 percent from the year before. That was worth 857 million dollars, a 23 percent decline. However, the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative imposed additional tariffs on July 24th. The pork industry is emphasizing that non-tariff barriers must also be resolved as part of any deal. A full recovery in exports will require resolving quarantine and sanitary and phytosanitary issues alongside any tariff changes.

Meanwhile, pig333 reports that China's pork imports fell sharply in the first half of 2026. Imports totaled 390,000 tons, down 26.4 percent from 530,000 tons in the same period last year. The decline is largely due to a surge in domestic Chinese production, which rose 4.2 percent year over year in the first quarter. In response, the Chinese government plans to cut its sow herd from 40.3 million head down to 39 million head to help rebalance supply and demand. Imports are expected to recover only gradually in the second half of the year, with prices likely staying at low levels. For pork exporters in the EU, the U.S., and Japan, a genuine rebound in Chinese demand remains a key point to watch for the rest of the year.

Also on the trade front, National Hog Farmer reports that Mexico's restrictions related to Pseudorabies Virus have sharply cut U.S. pork offal exports, even as overall trade with Mexico grows. Total U.S. pork exports in May rose 10 percent year over year to 245,874 metric tons, a strong number overall. But the drop in variety meat and offal exports weighed on the value added by those shipments. Mexico is one of the largest destinations for U.S. pork, so resolving these restrictions is essential for offal exports to recover. The National Pork Producers Council and the U.S. Meat Export Federation are pushing for a quick resolution through bilateral talks, and the outcome will directly affect industry revenue.

Now to a major packer investment. Smithfield Foods has announced a 1.3 billion dollar investment in a new pork processing plant in Sioux Falls, South Dakota. It is the largest corporate investment in the state's history. The new facility will be a state-of-the-art integrated processing and packaging plant covering 1.1 million square feet. It replaces an aging facility that has operated for 115 years. Construction is expected to begin in the first half of 2027, with operations starting by the end of 2028. That represents a significant long-term boost to U.S. pork processing capacity. Smithfield expects total capital expenditures for 2026 to fall between 350 million and 450 million dollars. The new plant is drawing attention as one of the packing industry's largest investments.

Let's finish with a look at market conditions in the European Union and China. The European Commission projects that EU pork production will fall 1 percent in 2026, to 21.76 million metric tons, mainly due to a decline in the sow herd. EU pork exports are also expected to decline, down 3 percent to 2.89 million metric tons, continuing a gradual decrease from 2.94 million metric tons in 2024. Supply pressure from African Swine Fever, which has kept Spanish pork inside the EU rather than exported, is weighing on prices across the bloc. EU producers face the challenge of balancing stable domestic consumption, with self-sufficiency holding around 115 percent, against the need to find new export markets.

According to AHDB, China's pork market continues to be weighed down by oversupply and weak demand. Analysts expect soft prices to persist through 2026 as rising domestic production improves self-sufficiency and reduces the need for imports. The Chinese government has introduced measures such as stockpiling purchases to help stabilize prices, though the effect so far has been limited. Exports from major EU producing countries such as Spain and Denmark to China are also expected to remain weak. Exporters targeting the Chinese market will likely need to keep diversifying toward alternative markets until demand recovers.

That wraps up today's major stories. USDA trimmed its production and price outlook in the July WASDE report, even as cutout values and lean hog futures held firm. Disease outbreaks of African Swine Fever and foot-and-mouth disease have reached record global levels, and the EU has updated its control zones in response. U.S.-China tariff talks are advancing, though Chinese import volumes remain sharply lower for the first half of the year. Mexico's PRV restrictions continue to cut into U.S. offal exports, and Smithfield Foods announced a 1.3 billion dollar investment in a new plant in South Dakota. Both the EU and China are facing softer market conditions heading into the rest of 2026.

That's your pork industry news for today.
