Monday, August 10th. Here is your pork industry news. Today we're covering falling hog futures and packer margins. We'll also look at a global supply glut that's weighing on prices worldwide. Then we turn to the ongoing African swine fever situation in Germany and the Philippines. And we close with a strong quarter for U.S. pork exports and for packer Tyson Foods.

We start with the U.S. market. CME lean hog futures for the August contract fell 1.1 cents to settle at 95.5 cents per pound. Remember, the CME lean hog contract is a live hog futures contract, named for the hog breed rather than for lean meat. The pork cutout price also dropped below 100 dollars this week. That's a sharp decline from last week's average of 102 dollars and 36 cents. Softening ham primal prices were the main driver of the drop. Weakness in the broader stock market and demand concerns added further pressure on the futures market. Weekly hog slaughter numbers rose from the previous week. Taken together, the market looks set to stay oversupplied in the near term.

That oversupply is also squeezing packers. U.S. pork packer margins are slipping as the cutout price falls below 100 dollars. That's narrowing the spread between carcass procurement costs and cutout revenue. Producer profits remain solid for now, but packers are facing pressure to adjust production and revisit shipping schedules. That could affect industry-wide profitability going forward. It may also have knock-on effects for future plant utilization rates and hiring plans across the packing sector.

Meanwhile, this summer's pork price rally ended earlier than usual, according to industry reporting. Carcass prices fell by more than 4 dollars over the past week. Hams, bellies, loins, and butts all topped out during that stretch. In China, pork supply remains oversupplied. Roughly 6 million sows are being culled there, a number that actually exceeds the entire U.S. sow herd. Brazil continues to push aggressive export volumes, adding even more pressure to global supply and demand. The seasonal low is now expected to arrive earlier than usual, in late August or early September. There's caution in the industry about how strong any autumn price recovery will actually be.

Turning to the bigger picture, RaboResearch's latest global pork market report points to stagnant demand and oversupply weighing on prices worldwide. China's pork imports for the first half of 2026 fell 30 percent year over year. That works out to about 314,000 tons, as excess domestic supply continues to limit purchases from abroad. The European Union is losing competitiveness due to African swine fever and export restrictions. Meanwhile Brazil and the United States are gaining export share at the EU's expense. A gradual recovery in Chinese imports is expected in the second half of the year as sow culling there progresses. Even so, analysts at RaboResearch say any real price rebound will likely be limited through the rest of 2026.

A separate outlook from Pig Progress on global 2026 pork production points to a similar turning point. Production grew in the first half of the year, driven by recovery in China and increased output in the U.S. and Brazil. But the second half is expected to bring a shift, as the oversupply eases and both production and prices reach an inflection point. The EU's exports remain constrained by African swine fever and rising structural costs, while Brazil and the U.S. keep expanding their share. Overall global trade volume for the year is expected to stay roughly flat, with production adjustments across countries determining where prices go next.

In Europe specifically, AHDB's latest EU pork market update shows prices trending softer year over year. Export restrictions tied to African swine fever in Spain have created surplus stock that's pressuring domestic prices. Trade flows are largely stable for now, but AHDB flags further spread of African swine fever as the biggest risk to market stability. EU pork production for 2026 is projected to fall 1 percent, with exports down 3 percent, as structural adjustment continues. A mid-term price floor could emerge if production cuts continue, though the outlook could shift significantly depending on how African swine fever spreads.

On the disease front, African swine fever continues to challenge Germany. Wild boar cases in North Rhine-Westphalia have now passed 500, with the latest count at 814 confirmed infections. Cases peaked in March 2026, when the state confirmed 123 new infections in a single month. No domestic pig herds have been infected so far, and containment efforts continue. In late July, the European Commission updated the geographic zoning for African swine fever control areas under regulation 2026/1908. The risk of spread to major hog-producing neighbors Belgium and the Netherlands remains a concern, and international markets continue to watch German pork with caution.

The cost of that containment effort in North Rhine-Westphalia has now reached 35 million euros. Roughly 8,000 wild boar have been culled as part of the response. Domestic pig herds in the state remain free of the virus. But North Rhine-Westphalia sits close to the Belgian and Dutch borders. That keeps the risk of further spread into those major hog-producing regions elevated. The rising containment cost is affecting Germany's competitiveness in the pork industry, and trading partners are watching closely.

In the Philippines, authorities have introduced new African swine fever import rules that allow for regionalization instead of full country-level import bans. Under the new approach, pork can be imported from disease-free certified regions even within countries that have reported African swine fever cases. That's seen as a potential opening for major European exporters like Spain to regain market access. Other Asian importers, including Japan and South Korea, may look to this policy shift as they consider similar reforms. Regionalized African swine fever import rules are becoming more common internationally and could be key to normalizing global trade flows.

On trade, USMEF reported that U.S. pork exports reached new highs in July. Mexico remained the largest market for U.S. pork, with volumes up year over year. Exports to Colombia also grew sharply compared with a year ago. Exports to China, however, remain at low levels because of retaliatory tariffs. Those tariffs now stand at an effective 47 percent. Recovery in the China market will depend heavily on progress in trade negotiations between the two countries. Beef and lamb exports were also strong in July, underscoring the overall competitiveness of U.S. meat exports right now. USMEF says market diversification remains central to the U.S. export strategy going forward.

In policy news, the stalled 2026 Farm Bill is creating real problems for U.S. pork producers, according to Pork Business. The Senate Agriculture Committee voted the bill down 10 to 11, leaving its future genuinely uncertain. A provision that would have created a single national standard for animal housing, replacing California's Prop 12 rules, is now in limbo as a result. That leaves producers facing a costly patchwork of state-by-state animal welfare regulations instead of one national rule. Industry groups are warning that the situation threatens producers' survival, language that underscores how high the stakes are seen to be. Those groups are now pushing lawmakers for a revote in September. The bill also includes a bipartisan provision on mandatory country-of-origin labeling, adding further complexity to the political negotiations ahead.

Finally, some corporate news. Tyson Foods reported strong third quarter results for fiscal 2026. Total sales rose 3.1 percent year over year to approximately 13.9 billion dollars. The pork segment stayed on track to meet its full-year adjusted operating income guidance, which the company has set at 250 to 300 million dollars. Adjusted earnings per share came in at 99 cents, just under analyst expectations for the quarter. That's in contrast to the beef segment, which posted a loss of 138 million dollars amid tight cattle supplies. The pork segment's steadiness stood out against that backdrop. Tyson maintained its full-year free cash flow forecast of 1.3 to 1.7 billion dollars, reaffirming the strategic importance of its pork business to the company overall.

That's your pork industry news for today.
