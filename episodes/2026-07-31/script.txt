Friday, July 31st. Here is your pork industry news. Today we cover strong U.S. hog prices and futures, and a serious global disease picture with rising African Swine Fever and foot-and-mouth risk. We also have a wave of trade news led by surging U.S. pork exports to Japan. Later, we'll check in on the United Kingdom market and a proposed change to U.S. processing rules. Let's start with prices and supply.

CME lean hog futures closed at 102.77 cents per pound on July 29th. Remember, lean hog futures track live hog prices, not pork meat prices, since the name refers to the hog breed itself. That closing level marks a gain of about 9 percent over the past month alone. The rally has also widened lean hogs' price advantage over cattle, which are facing their own separate pressures. USDA's national base hog price reached 101 dollars per hundredweight this week. Strong packer buying during the summer demand season is helping support that price level. Solid export flows are adding further support on top of that seasonal demand. Even so, current prices remain about 4 percent below where they stood a year ago. Heavier hog shipments expected this fall could put a cap on further price gains. Some analysts note that pork's relative affordability compared with beef may be drawing in more price-conscious consumers this summer.

A separate USDA daily livestock report for July 28th showed the pork cutout value at 101 dollars and 78 cents per hundredweight. That figure continues a firming trend that has held for most of the week. Cold storage pork stocks stood at 458.2 million pounds as of that report. That is up 9.4 percent from a year earlier, pointing to a modestly oversupplied market overall. Lower feed costs have encouraged producers to add more weight to hogs before shipment. That practice is adding further supply pressure on top of already ample cold storage stocks. Even with that supply pressure, exports remain solid heading into late summer. Grilling season demand is giving the industry a cautiously positive outlook for the weeks ahead.

Now let's turn to disease and biosecurity, where the picture remains serious worldwide. According to Pork Business, African Swine Fever has now been reported in more than 71 countries. The World Organisation for Animal Health puts cumulative infections since 2022 at 1.2 million pigs. Total losses from the disease have now topped 2.4 million head. Poland confirmed its first domestic African Swine Fever case of 2026. That outbreak is affecting around 21,000 pigs in the country. Germany's Saxony region has also reported a renewed outbreak of its own. Foot-and-mouth disease is spreading globally at the same time as African Swine Fever. Together, the two diseases are creating a double threat of production losses and export restrictions. The Swine Health Information Center is urging hog farmers everywhere to sharply strengthen their biosecurity measures.

The African Swine Fever outbreak confirmed in Spain in late 2025 continues to weigh heavily on European exports. Major importers including Japan and the Philippines are maintaining their bans on Spanish pork well into 2026. As a result, European Union-wide pork exports are projected to fall 8 percent this year. The bloc is now working to diversify into alternative markets to offset that decline. Rising feed costs are adding further pressure to producer margins across the region on top of the export losses. Spain's path back to competitiveness will depend on regaining its health certifications from importing countries. In the meantime, that gap is already creating export opportunities for U.S. and Canadian pork bound for Japan and Southeast Asia.

Let's move to trade, where the leading story is a strong surge in U.S. pork exports to Japan. U.S. pork exports to Japan topped 160,000 metric tons from January through May of 2026. Those shipments were worth 614 million dollars over that five month period. That represents a gain of about 20 percent from the same period a year earlier. Japan's import bans tied to the Spanish African Swine Fever outbreak have helped drive that increase. Demand has also been growing at Japanese convenience stores and restaurants. That includes ramen shops, which have become notable buyers of American pork. Japan has become the United States' third-largest pork export market, ranking behind only China and Mexico. The key question going forward is how the competitive landscape shifts once the Spanish outbreak is eventually brought under control.

On the other side of the trade picture, pig333 reports that China's pork imports fell 26.4 percent in the first half of 2026. Total imports over that period came to 390,000 tons. Rising domestic Chinese production is the main driver behind that drop. Weak domestic demand and high cold storage inventories in China are compounding the effect. The European Union, the United States, and Brazil, as major suppliers to China, are all feeling the impact of that pullback. Exporting countries are now being pushed to diversify their markets in response. The European Union faces a particularly tough squeeze here, given its overlapping troubles from the Spanish African Swine Fever outbreak. When Chinese demand recovers, and how quickly its stockpiles are drawn down, remain the key variables for global pork trade in the second half of the year.

Even so, U.S. pork exports to China climbed 33 percent in April compared with a year earlier. Easing trade friction between the two countries appears to have provided part of that boost. Seasonal demand inside China also played a role in the April jump. That increase came even though China's overall import volume was down for the first half of the year as a whole. That contrast raises the possibility that some importers front-loaded their purchases ahead of expected changes. Ongoing U.S.-China tariff policy will help determine whether that export momentum can be sustained. Domestic Chinese pork prices will also play a role in shaping that outcome.

Also on the trade front, National Hog Farmer reports that Mexico has lifted all of its Pseudorabies Virus restrictions on U.S. pork offal. Those restrictions had been costing the industry roughly 7 million dollars a week while they remained in place. Mexico is the largest destination for U.S. pork exports overall. Cumulative U.S. pork exports to Mexico already topped 1.09 billion dollars from January through May of 2026. Industry groups have strongly welcomed the removal of the restrictions. They are calling it a sign of stabilizing agricultural trade relations between the two countries.

Turning to the United Kingdom, AHDB's Pork Sector Council chair delivered an update this month. Rising slaughter numbers and heavier carcass weights have created an oversupply situation domestically. At the same time, European prices remain soft, and the combination is squeezing farmer margins. Even so, domestic consumption of British pork continues to trend higher. Exports of British pork are also continuing to trend higher alongside domestic demand. Production for 2026 is expected to reach about 1.03 million tonnes. That would mark an increase of 5 percent from last year's total. The council has approved a summer television advertising campaign to help boost demand further. Council officials expect the current market imbalance to continue in the near term. They are urging producers to strengthen their financial management as a result.

Finally, in the United States, USDA has proposed raising the limits on processing line speeds at pork plants. The change would expand processing capacity for packers across the industry. It would also help cut costs for those same packing operations. Smithfield Foods is among the major companies expected to benefit from the change. That benefit would come partly through shorter payback periods on equipment investments. Stronger export competitiveness is also expected to follow from the higher line speeds. Industry groups have welcomed the proposal as a straightforward efficiency gain. Animal welfare and food safety advocates have raised objections to the plan, however. Some labor unions have also submitted objections over the proposed change. The focus now shifts to how the final rule is written. Whether it clears Congress will also shape the outcome from here.

That's your pork industry news for today.
