Sunday, August 2nd. Here is your pork industry news. Today we lead with a new case of African Swine Fever in Finland, part of a wider pattern of disease risk spreading across more than 70 countries. We'll also cover firming and falling U.S. hog prices, a sharp slide in Chinese pork imports, and big trade and packer news out of Europe, the United States, and Japan. Let's start with disease and biosecurity.

Finland has confirmed its first-ever case of African Swine Fever. National Hog Farmer reports the virus was detected in three dead wild boars found near Virolahti, close to the Russian border. Finnish authorities confirmed the case on July 31st, a day after the animals were found. The area sits close to Poland and Estonia, both of which have already reported outbreaks of their own this year. Officials immediately imposed pig movement restrictions inside the infected zone. They have also banned wild boar hunting and feeding in the area. Industry groups are watching closely for any impact on Finland's pork exports. There is also concern this signals further spread of the disease across the European Union.

That concern is backed up by a broader surveillance report from the Swine Health Information Center, covered by Pork Business. The World Organisation for Animal Health has tracked more than 1.2 million African Swine Fever cases across 71 countries and regions since 2022. More than 2.4 million pigs have been lost to the disease over that period. Foot-and-mouth disease is also being reported at record levels alongside African Swine Fever. Finland's new incursion is cited as the latest example of the disease pushing into new territory. Industry leaders are calling for stronger vaccine development, earlier detection, and closer international cooperation. Trade restrictions and market disruption remain the biggest risks as both diseases keep spreading.

Turning to the U.S. market, Pork Business reports the pork cutout has turned higher, gaining nearly 2 dollars per hundredweight to a weekly average of 104 dollars and 11 cents. Belly prices led the move, rising 14 dollars and 23 cents. Ham prices also climbed, adding 7 dollars and 45 cents. The national average base hog price came in at 101 dollars and 71 cents per hundredweight. Packer losses are narrowing as seasonal demand strengthens. Where supply and slaughter capacity trends go from here will shape the market's next move.

Even so, CME lean hog futures fell to a two-week low on July 29th, closing at 102.77 cents per pound, the lowest level since July 14th. Remember, lean hog futures track live hog prices rather than pork meat prices, since the name refers to the hog breed itself. Prices had firmed earlier in the week on a seasonal demand rebound, then slipped back as that demand softened. The October contract dropped 2.35 cents to close at 83.35 cents per pound. USDA valued the pork carcass cutout that day at 101 dollars and 89 cents per hundredweight.

On the supply side, USDA's July WASDE report trimmed its 2026 U.S. pork production forecast to 28.135 billion pounds. National Hog Farmer says the cut mainly reflects a decline in farrowings shown in the latest hogs and pigs survey. That decline was partly offset by heavier expected carcass weights. USDA's 2027 forecast came in higher, at 28.255 billion pounds, a 1 percent increase from 2026. Improved sow productivity is expected to support both larger litters and higher marketings next year. USDA says the outlook points to tighter near-term pork supply conditions heading through the summer cutout season.

Feed costs, meanwhile, look set to stay favorable. The same July WASDE update, covered by pig333, projects corn in a range of 4 dollars and 25 cents to 4 dollars and 50 cents per bushel for the coming crop year. Soybean meal is projected in a range of 275 to 325 dollars per ton. Those lower feed costs should support producer profitability to some degree. Analysts caution that crop conditions in July and August could still push prices higher than currently expected. The report also flags a need for producers to weigh weather-related risk when planning trade and marketing strategy.

Turning to China, pig333 reports Chinese pork imports fell 26.4 percent in the first half of 2026, to 390,000 tons. Imports over just the January through May stretch came to 314,000 tons, a decline of 30 percent. Domestic oversupply and weak consumer demand are the main drivers behind that decline. Wholesale pork prices hit a 16 year low in June before rebounding about 7 percent in July. Even with that rebound, import demand remains scarce as long as the domestic glut persists.

Bloomberg, reporting on July 26th, describes China's broader pork market recovery as still struggling against tepid demand. Domestic hog prices have fallen below chicken prices in some markets, reflecting just how oversupplied the sector remains. That combination of oversupply and weak demand is pushing major exporters to reconsider their strategies for the Chinese market. The European Union and the United States are both among those affected. A short-term recovery in Chinese import demand looks unlikely under current conditions.

In Europe, China has issued its final ruling in an 18 month anti-dumping investigation into European Union pork. Pork Business reports the final tariffs range from 4.9 to 19.8 percent, down sharply from the provisional rates of 15.6 to 62.4 percent. Spain's Litera Meat received the lowest rate, at 4.9 percent, while Danish Crown was assigned the top rate of 19.8 percent. The investigation was widely seen as retaliation for the European Union's tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles. Even though the final rates came in milder than the industry expected, the structural decline in European exports to China is continuing. The mid to long term impact on the European Union's pork industry remains largely unchanged.

That pressure is already visible in European hog prices. Danish Crown cut its hog purchase price to 7.1 Danish kroner per kilogram on July 16th, roughly half of last year's level and the lowest in more than 20 years. The European Union's weighted average carcass price for week 26 came in at 160.1 euros per 100 kilograms, down more than 26 percent from a year earlier. Structural oversupply across the European Union is the main driver. That's compounded by the sharp drop in exports to China from Spain, the Netherlands, and Denmark following China's new anti-dumping tariffs. Danish Crown's management is warning that conditions could still get worse from here. That leaves the wider European Union pork sector under severe profit pressure heading into the fall.

On the packer front, Pork Business reports Smithfield Foods will build a new 1.3 billion dollar pork processing plant in Sioux Falls, South Dakota. The 1.1 million square foot facility is designed around advanced automation and an efficient process flow. Smithfield is positioning it as the most advanced pork processing facility in the country. The company expects the plant to begin operations by the end of 2028. Industry watchers see the investment as part of a broader push to boost competitiveness across the U.S. pork processing sector.

Separately, Tyson Foods has agreed to an 85 million dollar settlement in a long-running pork price-fixing lawsuit. The seven year case alleged that Tyson, JBS, Smithfield, and other major packers used an industry data platform called Agri Stats to restrict pork supply and inflate prices. The settlement is the largest in the case so far, surpassing Smithfield's earlier 75 million dollar settlement. Regulatory scrutiny of pricing practices and industry information sharing is likely to intensify from here.

Finally, in Japan, USMEF reports that U.S. pork exports to Japan are up nearly 20 percent so far in 2026. July alone saw shipments of 31,344 metric tons, up 7 percent from a year earlier. Those July shipments were worth 138.3 million dollars, up 8 percent from a year ago. The exit of Spanish pork from the Japanese market due to African Swine Fever has been the biggest tailwind behind that growth. Foodservice buyers, including ramen chains and bento makers, are increasingly turning to U.S. loin and shoulder cuts. A weaker yen remains a headwind for U.S. exporters. Even so, USMEF expects demand for U.S. pork in Japan to keep growing.

That's your pork industry news for today.
