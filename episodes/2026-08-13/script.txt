Let's start with prices and futures. According to The Pig Site, the CME lean hog August contract tracks live hog values rather than pork meat itself. That contract edged up 0.20 cents on August 10th to close at 95.70 cents per pound. That same day, the USDA's pork carcass cutout value came in at 101 dollars and 81 cents per hundredweight, up 31 cents from the previous session. Live cattle futures for August also gained ground, rising 1.575 cents to 233.275 cents per pound. Moving to the next session, Brownfield Ag News reported that the CME lean hog October contract slipped 0.35 cents on August 11th to close at 83.32 cents per pound. In the grain markets that same day, September corn futures fell 1.5 cents to 4 dollars and 36 and three-quarter cents per bushel. September soybean meal futures eased 50 cents to 305 dollars per ton. Together, the two sessions point to hog futures drifting lower even as cash cutout values held firm, with feed markets showing only modest movement. For producers watching the board day to day, it was a quiet stretch rather than a dramatic swing in either direction.

Turning to feed costs, The Pig Site reported that Ukraine's agriculture ministry issued two separate grain warnings this week. Both point to mounting pressure on the country's export capacity. First, the ministry cautioned that Ukraine could face a shortfall of up to 11 million tons of grain storage capacity this fall. The warning points to the loss of Black Sea port access, which normally handles ninety percent of the country's grain exports. Despite that disruption, Ukraine has already harvested more than 17 million tons of grain from about 34 percent of its planted area. Analysts still expect the 2026 harvest to exceed 60 million tons, with ending stocks reaching a record 14.3 million tons. Separately, the ministry cut its grain export forecast for the 2026 to 2027 marketing year, which runs from July through June, by as much as 12 percent. The new forecast calls for exports of 38 to 40 million tons, down from a previous target of 43 million tons. Shipments from the port of Odesa have nearly stopped since late July following Russian attacks on the facility. The ministry said losses to the agricultural sector from the port blockade could reach as much as 3 billion dollars. That figure underscores how exposed the region's feed grain supply chain remains heading into the fall marketing season.

On the disease front, The Pig Site reported that Hungary's food chain safety office confirmed a new African swine fever outbreak on a pig farm in Somogy county on August 10th. All 1,850 pigs on the farm will be culled as a result. This marks the second outbreak confirmed on a domestic pig farm in Hungary so far this year. Meanwhile, National Hog Farmer reported that the USDA and the U.S. Department of Homeland Security signed a memorandum of understanding to strengthen border biosecurity and combat livestock smuggling. The agreement addresses an estimated 800,000 cattle smuggled annually from Central America into Mexico. The two agencies said they will continue and expand their joint efforts to guard against biosecurity threats along the border. It's a reminder that disease prevention spans both wildlife-driven outbreaks in Europe and cross-border movement risks in North America.

Now to trade and exports. Euromeat News reported that China's pork and pork byproduct imports fell about 14 percent in the first half of 2026 to roughly 947,000 tons. Fresh and frozen pork cuts dropped sharply, down 28 percent to about 381,000 tons, while byproduct imports were nearly flat, down just 1 percent to about 566,000 tons. By country of origin, Spain remained the top supplier despite a 15 percent decline to about 230,000 tons. United States shipments fell 6 percent to about 160,000 tons. Denmark bucked the trend, rising 4 percent to about 91,000 tons, while Russia surged 93.4 percent to 49,000 tons, a sharp jump off a small base. In Brazil, ABPA reported that the SIAVS 2026 international animal protein trade show wrapped up in Sao Paulo. The ABPA pavilion generated an estimated 312.15 million dollars in export deals for Brazilian poultry and pork. More than 40 million dollars of that total was closed in just the first three days of the event. Separately, Suinocultura Industrial reported that Brazil's agriculture ministry continued diversifying its animal protein export markets. New facility approvals were announced for shipments to Japan and to the Eurasian Economic Union. Around the same time, Morocco suspended import tariffs on beef, sheep, goat, and camel meat. Those tariffs will remain suspended through December 2026. Together, these moves reflect Brazilian exporters finding new outlets even as sanitary risks in Europe, like the Hungary ASF case, draw attention elsewhere. It's a pattern worth watching as global buyers weigh supply security alongside price.

Turning to packer developments, The Pig Site reported that JBS posted record second-quarter revenue of 23.9 billion dollars, up 14 percent from a year earlier. Despite that record top line, the company reported a net loss of 102 million dollars for the quarter, compared with net income of 528 million dollars in the same period last year. The loss was driven largely by non-recurring items, including antitrust settlement costs. Adjusted EBITDA came in at 1.43 billion dollars, down 18.5 percent from a year earlier. JBS also announced a leadership change, naming Wesley Batista Filho as chief executive effective January 2027. The move marks the company's first leadership change in eight years and returns a member of the founding family to the top job. Separately, The Pig Site reported that Smithfield Foods cut its full-year 2026 guidance, citing cautious consumer spending and higher input costs. The company now expects roughly flat annual revenue, down from previous guidance calling for low single-digit growth. Adjusted operating profit guidance was cut to a range of 1.23 to 1.38 billion dollars, down from a prior range of 1.33 to 1.48 billion dollars. Even so, Smithfield's second-quarter revenue of 3.7 billion dollars beat analyst expectations of 3.68 billion dollars. Sales in its live hog production segment told a weaker story, falling 8.2 percent to 772 million dollars. That decline is another sign of continued softness in pork demand this year, even as the company's overall top line came in ahead of Wall Street expectations.

In country markets, Euromeat News reported that Germany's pig slaughter rose 0.5 percent in the first half of 2026 to 22.2 million head. Pork production was also up 0.5 percent over the same period, reaching 2.2 million tons. Domestically raised pigs accounted for 98.4 percent of that total, at 21.9 million head, while slaughter of foreign-raised pigs fell 14 percent to about 349,200 head. Pork remains Germany's largest meat category by far, making up 63.7 percent of the country's total meat production. And in a headline-only report, Pig Progress noted that a shrinking Danish pig sector could threaten Poland's efforts to rebuild its own herd numbers after recent declines. Full details behind that headline were not available at the time of publication, so we'll bring you more on that story as it develops.
